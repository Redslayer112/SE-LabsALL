Estimating with planning poker
Story points measure relative effort, not hours. The Fibonacci scale is used because the gaps widen as the numbers grow, which forces a decision instead of an argument between a 6 and a 7. In a team setting each member reveals a card at the same time so nobody anchors on the loudest voice; where the cards diverge, the two extremes explain their reasoning and the round is re-run.
DSM-6 is the only 8. Generating a weekly menu is not one operation: it is constraint solving over a recipe set, seven days of totals to balance, and a fallback when no compliant menu exists.
Prioritising and ranking the backlog
Priority follows user impact, not convenience. A requirement whose failure is a clinical safety incident outranks one whose failure is an inconvenience, which puts allergen exclusion (DSM-7) and dietitian approval (DSM-9) at High.
The backlog was then reordered so that rank matches priority: all six High stories above the three Medium ones, dependency order preserved inside each band. Both sprints pull from the top of that list. One consequence is that DSM-5 is Medium, so it ranks below all six High stories and lands in Sprint 2 despite sharing an epic with DSM-4.

5. Reflection
Did the estimations reflect the actual effort?
Mostly, with one miss. The 3s and 5s behaved as estimated. DSM-6 at 8 was under-estimated rather than mis-sized: the 8 signalled uncertainty, but the estimate still assumed plan generation was a single story. It was not, and it should have been split. Once it was split in Sprint 2 it finished without difficulty.
A large Fibonacci number is a signal to split a story, not a label for a big one.
Was the backlog well-prioritised?
Yes. Priority followed user impact, the backlog was ranked to match, and both sprints pulled from the top. The two safety-critical stories, DSM-7 and DSM-9, shipped inside the first two sprints; the two still outstanding are both Medium. Nothing High is left undone.
One ordering choice is arguable. DSM-8 was pulled into Sprint 1 alongside the plan generation it depends on, so when DSM-6 slipped it shipped into a system with no plans to review. Ranking by impact is right; sequencing a dependent story into the same sprint as its dependency is a risk that should have been visible at planning time.
How did the simulated sprint align with the plan?
Sprint 1 did not: 26 committed, 18 delivered, with the largest story missing. Sprint 2 aligned exactly at 16 and 16, partly because it was planned after watching Sprint 1 miss.
Two sprints is too small a sample for a stable velocity, but 18 and 16 suggest a real capacity near 17 points, against the 26 initially committed.

The line stops at 8 and never reaches the axis. That gap is the capacity finding: a commitment roughly 44% above what was delivered.
The plotted curve is less informative, and the reason matters. The To Do → In Progress → Done moves were all made inside one session, so every event lands in the first few minutes of a seven-day axis and the line renders as a near-vertical drop rather than a staircase beside the guideline. That is an artifact of the simulation, not a property of the plan.
In a real sprint that shape would be a warning sign, and for one story it was: DSM-6 was too large to close incrementally.
What the team would take into the next sprint
Split anything estimated at 8 or above before committing to it. DSM-6 is the whole argument.
Do not put a story in the same sprint as the work it depends on unless that dependency finishes early. DSM-8 delivered nothing usable for a week.
Commit to about 17 points, not 26, until there is velocity history to justify more.
