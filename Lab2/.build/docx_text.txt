Estimating with planning poker
Story points measure relative effort, not hours. Complexity, volume of work and uncertainty all feed into one number. The Fibonacci scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 …) is used deliberately: the gaps widen as the numbers grow, which forces a decision rather than letting the team argue between a 6 and a 7 on work nobody understands well enough to separate.
In a team setting each story would be read out, briefly discussed, and every member would reveal a card at the same time so that nobody anchors on the loudest voice. Where the cards agree the estimate is taken; where they diverge the two extremes explain their reasoning and the round is re-run. The discussion exists to surface the hidden assumption causing the spread, which is usually where the risk in the story sits. Converging on a number is secondary.
Applying that here, DSM-6 is the only 8 in the backlog. Generating a weekly menu is not one operation: it is constraint solving over a recipe set, seven days of totals to balance, and a fallback path when no compliant menu exists. That uncertainty, not raw size, is what pushed it a full Fibonacci step above the 5s. The estimate is revisited in the reflection below, because DSM-6 is the story that did not finish.
Prioritising and ranking the backlog
Priority follows user impact, not convenience: a requirement whose failure is a clinical safety incident outranks one whose failure is an inconvenience. That puts allergen exclusion (DSM-7) and dietitian approval (DSM-9) at High, and the fulfilment-record and cut-off stories at Medium.
The backlog was then reordered so that rank matches priority. All six High stories sit above the three Medium ones, with dependency order preserved inside each band (a profile must exist before a plan can be generated from it, and plans must exist before a dietitian can review them). Both sprints then pull from the top of that ranked list instead of cherry-picking, which is what keeps the ranking meaningful.
One consequence is visible in the plan: DSM-5 Update health profile is Medium, so it ranks below all six High stories and lands in Sprint 2 even though it belongs to the same epic as DSM-4, which shipped in Sprint 1.

5. Reflection
Did the estimations reflect the actual effort?
Mostly, with one clear miss. The 3s and 5s behaved as estimated. The profile story, the allergen check and the two dietitian stories were well understood, and nothing surprising surfaced while working them. DSM-6 at 8 points was the exception, under-estimated rather than mis-sized: the 8 was chosen to signal "this one is uncertain", but the estimate still rested on an assumption that plan generation could be treated as a single story. It could not.
It should have been split. Generating a candidate menu, balancing daily totals against the profile, and handling the case where no compliant menu exists are three separable pieces of work, and splitting them would have let part of the value land inside Sprint 1 instead of the whole story sliding. That is exactly what happened once it was split in Sprint 2, where it finished without difficulty.
A large Fibonacci number is a signal to split a story, not a label for a big one.
Was the backlog well-prioritised?
Yes, and the outcome supports it. Priority followed user impact, the backlog was ranked to match, and both sprints pulled from the top of that ranking. The stories whose failure would be a clinical safety incident, allergen exclusion (DSM-7) and dietitian approval (DSM-9), were High and shipped inside the first two sprints. The two stories still outstanding (DSM-11 cut-off enforcement, DSM-12 delivery-outcome recording) are both Medium. Nothing High is left undone.
One ordering choice is arguable in hindsight. DSM-8, the dietitian's review queue, was pulled into Sprint 1 alongside the plan generation it depends on. When DSM-6 slipped, DSM-8 shipped into a system with no plans to review: three points of finished work that delivered no usable value until the following sprint. Ranking by impact is right; sequencing a dependent story into the same sprint as the work it depends on is a risk that should have been visible at planning time.
How did the simulated sprint align with the plan?
Sprint 1 did not align: 26 points committed, 18 delivered, with the single largest story missing entirely. Sprint 2 aligned exactly, 16 committed and 16 delivered, but partly because it was planned after watching Sprint 1 miss, and sized more conservatively with the carry-over taken first.
The plan improved because the first sprint's failure informed it, which is the feedback loop Scrum exists to create. Two sprints is far too small a sample to call a stable velocity, but 18 and 16 are close enough to suggest a real capacity somewhere near 17 points per sprint, against the 26 initially committed.

The line stops at 8 and never reaches the axis. That gap is the capacity finding: 26 points committed, 8 still open when the time-box expired, a commitment roughly 44% above what was actually delivered.
The plotted curve itself is less informative, and the reason matters. The sprint was given the one-week duration the handout asks for, but the To Do → In Progress → Done moves were all made inside a single lab session, so every event lands in the first few minutes of a seven-day axis and the remaining-work line renders as a near-vertical drop at the left edge instead of a staircase descending beside the grey guideline. That is an artifact of simulating a week's work in one sitting, not a property of the plan.
A real sprint showing this shape, nothing moving for most of the time-box and then everything closing at once, would be a genuine warning sign. For one story it actually was. DSM-6 was too large to close incrementally, which is precisely the failure the handout describes as "a sharp drop during the sprint … work has not been estimated accurately, or broken down properly."
Read together with the velocity figures, the practical conclusion is that 26 points was an over- commitment, and planning the next sprint around 16–18 points would be the defensible forecast.
What the team would take into the next sprint
Three things, in order of how much they would change the outcome:
Split anything estimated at 8 or above before committing to it. DSM-6 is the whole argument.
Do not put a story in the same sprint as the work it depends on unless the dependency is scheduled to finish early in that sprint. DSM-8 delivered nothing usable for a week.
Commit to about 17 points, not 26, until there is enough velocity history to justify more.
